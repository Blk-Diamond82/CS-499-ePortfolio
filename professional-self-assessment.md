# Professional Self-Assessment

## Chanelle Harris

Technology has always been something I was naturally drawn to, although it took me some time to recognize that it could become more than a personal interest. My career and educational direction changed several times, beginning with business administration and later moving into medical administration. Throughout those experiences, however, one pattern continued to follow me: whenever a computer or technology issue came up, I found myself volunteering to troubleshoot it. I enjoyed taking something that was not working, investigating the problem, and figuring out how to make it work again. Eventually, coworkers began coming to me with computer issues before contacting IT. That was when I started to recognize that my natural fascination with computers and problem-solving might be worth exploring more seriously.

What drew me toward those problems was never just technology itself. I enjoy the puzzle. I like being presented with something that does not immediately have an obvious answer and having to dig deeper to understand what is happening. Troubleshooting gives me an opportunity to investigate different possibilities, test solutions, learn from what does not work, and eventually develop a viable solution. Once I recognized how consistently I gravitated toward that type of work, I began to wonder what I could accomplish if I stopped treating those abilities as something I simply happened to be good at and instead invested the time and effort into developing them. Pursuing computer science became my opportunity to find out.

When I entered the Computer Science program, I originally believed programming would be the natural career path. I had always considered attention to detail one of my strengths—until I started coding. Programming quickly taught me that attention to detail in software development exists on an entirely different level. A missing character, an incorrect variable, or a small error in logic can prevent an entire application from behaving as expected. Coding required a level of sustained focus and precision that I had underestimated. Learning to work through those challenges strengthened my patience, troubleshooting abilities, and willingness to methodically investigate problems rather than immediately looking for the fastest solution.

At the same time, learning to program helped me recognize something important about my interests. I could learn to code and appreciate the problem-solving involved, but writing code itself was not the part of technology that had originally inspired me. What had always captured my attention was understanding how technology works as a system—what happens when something fails, how different components interact, how to isolate the source of a problem, and what needs to change to restore or improve functionality. Programming became an important skill in my technical toolbox, but the Computer Science program helped me understand that computer science extends far beyond becoming a traditional software developer.

The program gave structure and technical depth to instincts I already had. Troubleshooting became debugging. Tinkering became designing, developing, and testing computing solutions. Finding a way to make something work developed into evaluating whether a solution was maintainable, efficient, secure, and appropriate for the problem being solved. Through coursework involving software engineering, algorithms and data structures, databases, cybersecurity, mobile applications, and full-stack development, I gained a much broader understanding of how computing systems are designed and how the individual components work together.

## Collaboration and Communication

My professional experience has strongly influenced how I approach technology. Working in leadership and operational environments has taught me that solving a problem is only part of delivering an effective solution. Technical information must also be communicated in a way that allows different stakeholders to understand the problem, make decisions, and take action. My previous experience in business and administrative environments strengthened my communication, organization, decision-making, and ability to understand the needs of different audiences.

The Computer Science program allowed me to apply those existing strengths in a technical environment. Throughout the program, I communicated technical information through software design documents, diagrams, project narratives, presentations, code reviews, and application demonstrations. These experiences required me to adjust how I communicated depending on whether the intended audience needed implementation-level technical detail or a broader explanation of how the solution addressed a business or user requirement.

CS 499 strengthened this skill further by requiring me to critically evaluate my own work and communicate not only what I developed, but why particular design decisions were appropriate. This has reinforced my understanding that effective computing solutions are rarely developed in isolation. Collaboration requires listening to different perspectives, communicating technical constraints clearly, incorporating feedback, and ensuring that technical decisions support the needs of the organization and the people using the solution.

## Software Engineering

Software engineering has become one of the strongest areas represented by my coursework. Across the program, I worked with multiple programming languages, development environments, and application architectures. My projects progressed from individual programs into applications containing interconnected components, interfaces, data models, APIs, and persistent storage.

The Travlr Getaways full-stack application demonstrates this progression particularly well. Throughout its development, the application evolved from static content and local data into a structured full-stack solution. Enhancing the application required me to think beyond whether individual features worked and evaluate the architecture supporting them. Separation of concerns, modularity, maintainability, reusable components, error handling, and the relationships between application layers became increasingly important as the system grew.

This experience changed the way I evaluate software. Functional code is important, but functional code is not necessarily finished or well-designed software. I now consider whether another developer could understand and maintain the solution, whether components are unnecessarily dependent on one another, how the application will respond when something fails, and whether the architecture provides room for future growth.

## Algorithms and Data Structures

My coursework also strengthened my understanding of algorithms and data structures and their role in solving computing problems efficiently. Earlier in the program, it was easy to think about data primarily as information stored or displayed by an application. My coursework taught me to think more critically about how information is organized, searched, sorted, filtered, and transformed.

During the Algorithms and Data Structures enhancement of the Travlr application, I applied these concepts to the administrative interface through search, filtering, and sorting functionality. The enhancement required me to consider how users interact with a collection of trip records and how the application should process that collection to produce meaningful results.

More importantly, I learned that algorithmic decision-making involves trade-offs. The most complex solution is not automatically the best solution. Efficiency must be considered alongside readability, maintainability, expected data size, user requirements, and the environment in which the solution will operate. This has strengthened my ability to evaluate multiple approaches instead of simply implementing the first solution that produces the correct output.

## Databases and Data Integrity

Database development has been another important area of growth. My coursework introduced me to persistent data management and helped me understand the relationship between an application's database, business logic, and user-facing functionality. The Travlr application uses MongoDB and Mongoose to manage persistent trip information, and the database enhancement allowed me to examine that layer much more critically.

During the enhancement, I strengthened the Trip schema through required-field validation and uniqueness constraints and improved how the API responds to different database conditions. One of the most valuable lessons occurred during negative testing. When I intentionally attempted to update a trip using a code that already belonged to another record, MongoDB correctly rejected the operation through its unique index. The application initially interpreted the duplicate-key condition as a generic server failure. By examining the error, identifying MongoDB error code 11000, and updating the controller logic, I was able to translate that database condition into a meaningful 409 Conflict response.

That experience reinforced that reliable database development requires more than successful CRUD operations. The database itself must protect the integrity of persisted information, while the application must handle database conditions in a predictable and useful manner. I learned to think of a schema as an enforceable contract for data rather than simply a description of fields. Combining application-level validation with database-level constraints produced a more reliable solution and demonstrated the importance of deliberately testing both successful and unsuccessful operations.

## Security Mindset

Security has increasingly become part of how I evaluate computing solutions rather than something I consider only after development is complete. Throughout the Computer Science program, I gained experience examining vulnerabilities, secure coding practices, data protection, authentication, and the importance of anticipating unintended or adversarial behavior.

The program taught me to ask more questions when evaluating a solution. What happens when unexpected input is received? What information should a user be permitted to access? What happens when an operation fails? Can malformed or duplicate information compromise the integrity of the system? Are errors handled in a way that protects the application while still providing useful information?

My database enhancement reinforced that security also includes protecting the integrity and reliability of application data. Required fields, controlled updates, uniqueness constraints, and predictable error handling reduce opportunities for malformed, duplicate, or inconsistent information to enter the system. Developing a security mindset has changed the way I approach software because I now consider failure conditions and misuse as part of the design process rather than only testing whether the intended path works correctly.

## ePortfolio and Professional Growth

The artifacts included in this ePortfolio demonstrate the progression of these skills rather than serving as isolated examples of completed assignments. The code review establishes a starting point by evaluating the Travlr application's existing architecture and identifying areas where it could be improved. The Software Design and Engineering enhancement demonstrates architectural growth and improved separation between application components. The Algorithms and Data Structures enhancement demonstrates how data-processing techniques can improve application functionality and usability. The Database enhancement demonstrates stronger persistence, validation, data integrity, and error management. Together, the artifacts and their supporting narratives demonstrate my ability to examine an existing computing solution, identify limitations, implement improvements, test those improvements, and evaluate the resulting solution.

The portfolio also represents an important change in how I evaluate my own technical work. Earlier in the program, completing a project successfully often meant reaching the point where the program produced the expected result. My perspective has developed beyond that standard. I now consider architecture, maintainability, efficiency, security, testing, data integrity, usability, and the needs of future developers and stakeholders who may interact with a system.

Perhaps the most important outcome of the Computer Science program has been gaining a better understanding of where I fit within technology. Learning to program did not convince me that I needed to spend my entire career writing software. Instead, it gave me a greater appreciation for how software works from the inside and helped me identify the areas of technology that consistently hold my interest. I am most engaged when I am troubleshooting, working across different components of a system, understanding how systems communicate, evaluating security and reliability, and determining why something is not behaving as expected.

My interests have therefore increasingly moved toward the intersection of technology, systems, infrastructure, troubleshooting, and leadership. I do not view that direction as moving away from computer science. I see it as gaining enough exposure to the field to understand where within it my strengths and interests are best aligned. My technical education allows me to understand the systems and software supporting an organization, while my professional experience allows me to consider the people, processes, and operational requirements surrounding those systems.

Looking back, my earlier experiences in business and medical administration were not unrelated detours from computer science. Each contributed skills that I continue to use today. Computer science added the technical foundation that had been missing. Together, those experiences have shaped how I approach technology: understand the problem, understand how the components interact, understand the people or organization affected by it, and develop a solution that is both technically sound and genuinely useful.

Completing CS 499 has allowed me to view the Computer Science program as a connected body of knowledge rather than a collection of individual courses. Software engineering, algorithms, databases, security, communication, and collaboration are interconnected parts of creating effective computing solutions. The artifacts contained in this ePortfolio demonstrate the technical skills I developed, but they also represent something more personal: the decision to take a natural curiosity for technology and intentionally develop it into a professional capability. I began by being the person willing to investigate when a computer stopped working. I am completing the program with a much deeper understanding of the systems behind those problems, the tools available to solve them, and the direction in which I want to continue growing.
